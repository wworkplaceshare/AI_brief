Apps
Sep 22

Amazon blocks Meta's Muse; six banks warn two days later

On Sep 22 six banks — Bank of America, Capital One, NatWest, ING, Commonwealth Bank of Australia and ASB — published "Building Trust in Agentic Commerce", warning that agentic checkout raises fraud, privacy and liability problems, and setting out five principles: transparency, safety, privacy and data, choice, and interoperability.

Amazon had begun blocking Muse from shopping on its site on the night of Sep 20, on three grounds: no authorisation, the agent not identifying itself as AI, and the alleged capture and storage of account credentials.

Muse hit about 1.8m iOS downloads and 642,000 daily actives in the US and Canada in its first 12 days, both ahead of ChatGPT's comparable window; Meta shares rose more than 20% in two weeks, adding over $200bn. Amazon is roughly 37-40% of US e-commerce. A researcher also disclosed a Muse Mac client flaw hijackable with a single command, which Meta patched by Sep 22.

Why it matters: The first real gate on agent commerce is not model capability but platform admission: identity disclosure, third-party credential custody and observable behaviour. Miss one and the door stays shut.

Policy
Sep 25

Appeals court backs the Pentagon: Claude stays out of DoD work

On Sep 25 the D.C. Circuit ruled in Anthropic v. Department of War, leaving in place the treatment of Claude as a supply-chain risk. The case is being heard as federal administrative law rather than a contract dispute.

More than 15 amicus briefs backed the petitioner, including 149 former judges, the ACLU and former Defense Secretary Leon Panetta. The dispute traces to Anthropic's refusal to allow Claude for mass surveillance or fully autonomous weapons.

An earlier piece of the same accountability line: on Sep 21, before this window, British Columbia sued OpenAI and Sam Altman in California federal court, alleging a failure to report risk before a February 2026 school shooting — the first judicial test of a management-responsibility doctrine.

Why it matters: A year ago these disagreements lived in contract terms; now they are public court records — a model's usage policy is being litigated as administrative law.

Models
Sep 23

Anthropic opens a Bay Area wet lab: 950 Claude agents surface a CRISPR-like enzyme system

The only instruction was to find interesting reverse transcriptases in DNA databases. About 950 agents ran for 21 hours and spent 210m tokens, narrowing 200,000-plus candidates to 20, each with a human-readable report.

One agent spotted an evenly spaced tandem-repeat array next to a phage sequence; the team named it ART. The preprint reports 3–21 short repeat copies that express as a set of short RNAs.

In parallel, Stanford's virtual biotech ran 37,000 agents to analyse about 50,000 clinical trials in under a week. Its proposed B7-H3 lung-cancer ADC was reached independently by a drugmaker months later and won FDA Breakthrough Therapy designation.

Why it matters: The bottleneck moved: hypothesis generation is collapsing in cost, validation is not — ART's function is unproven and every wet-lab step was done by humans.

Cloud & Capital
Sep 24

Two infrastructure megadeals in one week: Akamai's $11.6bn CPU contract, Nscale's $3.36bn convertible

On Sep 24 Akamai signed a seven-year, $11.6bn cloud contract with Anthropic — the largest in its history — expandable by $9bn to about $20bn. Akamai expects roughly $5.5bn of capex plus about $1.7bn more in 2026 to pre-purchase components including memory; revenue starts in the second half of 2027 and reaches roughly a $1.7bn annual run rate by the end of 2028.

What the contract buys is CPUs, not GPUs: the layer where agents call tools, open browsers and run code sits on general-purpose compute, and one task can mean a hundred such steps. In exchange Akamai issued Anthropic a warrant for up to about 5% of its common stock at $111.33 a share, vesting roughly 1% per additional $3bn committed. Akamai shares rose more than 17% after hours.

A day later UK AI cloud provider Nscale closed $3.36bn in convertible loan notes led by Third Point: a $2.36bn first tranche at closing plus a $1bn commitment from Nvidia expected in mid-November, converting automatically on completion of its IPO (into non-voting shares for Nvidia). It self-reports over $103bn in contracted value and filed its S-1 on Sep 18.

Why it matters: Both are priced on commitments rather than revenue, and both land as compute demand shifts from training toward the agent execution layer: one is a seven-year CPU contract, the other a bridge to an IPO.
