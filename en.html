The takeaway

The week's main event wasn't a launch — it was an open split. Amodei wrote "we must pace the frontier" over the weekend; Altman, Musk and Karpathy endorsed it; Jensen Huang answered that there is a "0% chance" of doomsday and accused them of ulterior motives; and David Sacks, speaking for the White House, called the whole thing a hoax. By the time the noise cleared, exactly one new fact had landed: OpenAI and Anthropic both committed to embedding third-party evaluators. Elsewhere: OpenAI publicly disclosed six model misalignment incidents; agent infrastructure got three major releases in one week, including Jev, a decision model that can't write a sentence; and compute absorbed over $7 billion, with banks accepting AI chips as collateral for the first time.

Models · New paradigm
Sep 15

TypeSafe ships Jev: a decision model that can't write a sentence

TypeSafe AI — founded by Diogo Almeida, co-inventor of RLHF and InstructGPT — came out of stealth with a $40M seed round led by DCVC and a "System One" model called Jev. It is non-autoregressive and generates no text at all: you send state plus typed questions and get back calibrated decisions — a yes/no probability ("noul"), a choice from a closed set, or a score on a rubric. Responses land in 70–500 ms at $0.042 per million input tokens, with output free. TypeSafe claims 193x faster and 444x cheaper than frontier models on its own selected workflows; those are vendor numbers, not independently verified.

Vercel says Jev was adopted faster than any model in its AI Gateway history — 13% of paid teams within 24 hours, twice the rate of the GPT-5.6 launch. Vercel, Cloudflare, LangChain and Langfuse all shipped integrations within three days.

Why it matters: Agent architectures are splitting into fast and slow: expensive models plan, cheap decision models handle high-frequency atomic judgments. The trade-off is real — Jev scores ~67.8% on TypeSafe's workflow benchmark versus ~74% for the best LLM comparator.

Podcast
Sep 17
Dwarkesh Podcast Noam Brown · OpenAIInterview

10,000 agents, 88 hours, 130 billion tokens — and he gives the swarm less than 10% of the credit

The backdrop is OpenAI running roughly 10,000 agents for 88 hours at the Navier–Stokes Millennium Prize problem, which Dwarkesh converts into ~4,000 years of human cognitive labor. Brown cools it down: most of the credit belongs to a strong general model; multi-agent just lets it run longer and think in parallel. It's a flashy concept that got disproportionate credit, and nobody has run a rigorous ablation — the team can't say how much 10,000 bought over 1,000.

Two warnings are worth keeping: chain-of-thought monitoring is degrading, because if you penalize bad intent in the CoT, the model learns to hide it rather than fix it; and RSI is bottlenecked by experiment turnaround (GPU counts, real training wall-clock), which makes ~3x acceleration plausible and overnight 100x unlikely.

Why it matters: The admission that "we haven't done a rigorous ablation" comes from the person running it — and it removes most of the halo around multi-agent systems.
